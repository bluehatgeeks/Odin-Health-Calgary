Short answer

To convert total testosterone, multiply nmol/L by 28.84 to get ng/dL, or divide ng/dL by 28.84 to get nmol/L. For free testosterone, 1 pg/mL equals 3.467 pmol/L. So the American Urological Association’s 300 ng/dL low-T cut-off is about 10.4 nmol/L on a Canadian report. Normal testosterone levels: Canada in nmol/L, the USA in ng/dL

There is no single normal number, so it helps to know the reference points behind the plain-words check. The Endocrine Society’s 2018 guideline gives a harmonized range of 264 to 916 ng/dL (9.2 to 31.8 nmol/L) for healthy, non-obese men aged 19 to 39 on CDC-certified assays. The American Urological Association (2018) uses a total T below 300 ng/dL on two early-morning tests, with symptoms. The Canadian Urological Association (2021) treats below 10 nmol/L as a reasonable threshold, and the European Male Ageing Study (2010) used below 11 nmol/L with a free T below 220 pmol/L and at least three sexual symptoms. Canadian labs print their own adult ranges: Alberta Precision Laboratories lists 8.0 to 35.0 nmol/L for men 18 and over, and the LifeLabs reference lab in British Columbia lists 8.4 to 28.8 nmol/L for men over 18. Always read the range printed on your own report first. Ranges in brackets below are our conversions.

Why Canada reports nmol/L and the USA ng/dL

It comes down to the measuring system each country’s labs adopted. Canada moved health care lab reporting to SI units, the modern metric system that counts molecules in moles per litre, in the early 1980s; a 1986 JAMA report by McQueen describes seven provinces, including Ontario and Alberta, converting in 1983. The United States never made that switch nationwide. In a 1987 Annals of Internal Medicine editorial, Edward Huth wrote that most of the US medical community had stood by while the rest of the medical world, including neighbouring Canada, moved to SI units, and in 2013 the FDA still described conventional units as the ones most US care teams are trained in. So a US report gives testosterone as mass per volume (nanograms per decilitre) and a Canadian report as amount per volume (nanomoles per litre). The hormone is the same: testosterone weighs 288.4 grams per mole (PubChem), which is where the 28.84 factor comes from. Free T follows the same logic one size smaller, pmol/L in Canada and pg/mL in the USA. If you have moved between countries, or use a US online lab while living in Canada, put every result in one unit before you compare.

How the free testosterone calculation works

Most free T results are calculated, not measured. Vermeulen, Verdonck and Kaufman (Journal of Clinical Endocrinology & Metabolism, 1999) found free T calculated from total T and SHBG was almost identical to equilibrium dialysis, the reference method, in every condition they studied except pregnancy. The Endocrine Society accepts a formula that reflects dialysis and says direct analog free T tests are inaccurate. The equation splits testosterone into three pools: held tightly by SHBG (binding constant 1,000,000,000 L/mol), held loosely by albumin (36,000 L/mol, with albumin weighing 69,000 g/mol) and free. It solves the balance between them as a quadratic. Bioavailable T is the free part plus the albumin-bound part. In the worked example published with the ISSAM calculator, total T 10 nmol/L (288 ng/dL), SHBG 40 nmol/L and albumin 4.3 g/dL give a free T of about 174 pmol/L (50 pg/mL), 1.74 percent of the total, and a bioavailable T of 118 ng/dL. Our calculator returns the same numbers. A calculation is only as good as its inputs, and de Ronde and colleagues (Clinical Chemistry, 2006) found that different published formulas give different results, so compare yours with the range from the lab and method that produced it.

Frequently asked questions

How do I convert testosterone from ng/dL to nmol/L?

Divide ng/dL by 28.84. To go the other way, multiply nmol/L by 28.84. The factor comes from the molecular weight of testosterone, 288.4 grams per mole (PubChem). For example, 300 ng/dL is about 10.4 nmol/L, 500 ng/dL is about 17.3 nmol/L, and 15 nmol/L is about 433 ng/dL. For free testosterone, multiply pg/mL by 3.467 to get pmol/L, so 64 pg/mL is about 222 pmol/L, which the Endocrine Society rounds to 220.

What is a normal testosterone level in nmol/L in Canada?

Each lab sets its own range. Alberta Precision Laboratories lists 8.0 to 35.0 nmol/L for men 18 and over, and the LifeLabs reference laboratory in British Columbia lists 8.4 to 28.8 nmol/L for men over 18. The Endocrine Society harmonized range for healthy, non-obese men aged 19 to 39 is 9.2 to 31.8 nmol/L (264 to 916 ng/dL), and the Canadian Urological Association treats a total T below 10 nmol/L as a reasonable threshold for low T, alongside symptoms. Compare your result with the range printed on your own report first.

How is free testosterone calculated?

Most labs calculate it from total testosterone, SHBG and albumin with the Vermeulen equation (1999), which matched equilibrium dialysis closely in the original study. The equation uses fixed binding strengths for SHBG and albumin and solves for the unbound part. In the worked example published with the ISSAM calculator, total T 10 nmol/L, SHBG 40 nmol/L and albumin 4.3 g/dL give a free T of about 174 pmol/L, 1.74 percent of the total. Albumin of 4.3 g/dL (43 g/L) is the default when no albumin result is available.

What is a normal free testosterone level in pmol/L?

There is no single harmonized range: the Endocrine Society notes that labs set their own limits and results depend on the method. The European Male Ageing Study (New England Journal of Medicine, 2010) used a free T below 220 pmol/L (64 pg/mL), with total T below 11 nmol/L and at least three sexual symptoms, to define late-onset hypogonadism. The LifeLabs reference laboratory in British Columbia lists a calculated free T range of 115 to 577 pmol/L for men. Check the unit and the method on your report before you compare.

Why do Canadian and US labs use different testosterone units?

Canada moved health care lab reporting to SI units, which count molecules in moles per litre, in the early 1980s, so total testosterone is reported in nmol/L and free T in pmol/L. The United States kept conventional mass units, so its reports show ng/dL and pg/mL. It is the same hormone measured with a different ruler: 1 nmol/L equals 28.84 ng/dL. We line up results from both countries in one unit when we review your labs with you.
