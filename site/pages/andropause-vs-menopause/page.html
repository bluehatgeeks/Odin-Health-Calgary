Andropause vs menopause: what is the difference?

Short answer

Menopause is a defined end point; andropause is a slow drift. In menopause the ovaries run out of follicles, estrogen falls and periods stop for good, on average at 51, and it happens to every woman. In men, testosterone falls gradually, about 1 percent a year from around 30 to 40, with no sudden stop, and only some men develop symptoms with truly low levels (late-onset hypogonadism). Menopause is usually recognized from symptoms and periods; low T needs symptoms plus two early-morning blood tests. Timing: a slow drift versus a defined end point

Menopause has a date. The Canadian Menopause Society defines natural menopause as the permanent end of periods caused by the loss of ovarian follicles, which brings low estrogen and the end of natural fertility. It is confirmed in hindsight, after twelve months in a row without a period, and the average age in Canada is 51, usually between 45 and 55; the US National Institutes of Health gives the same average. The run-up, called perimenopause, can begin several years earlier as estrogen and progesterone start to fluctuate. Male hormonal aging has no equivalent date. The NHS calls “male menopause” a misleading label because testosterone does not drop suddenly in midlife: it falls steadily, by about 1 percent a year from around age 30 to 40, and that slow decline is unlikely to cause problems by itself.

  • Menopause: one point in time

    Confirmed after twelve months without a period, on average at 51 in Canada and the USA.

  • Perimenopause comes first

    Hormones swing for several years before the final period, which is when many symptoms start.

  • Men: a gradual slope

    Testosterone eases down about 1 percent a year from around 30 to 40, with no sudden drop.

  • Symptoms are not automatic

    The slow decline alone rarely causes problems. Weight, sleep and health often matter more.

Not every man goes through andropause

Every woman reaches menopause. Symptomatic low testosterone in men is far less universal. The European Male Ageing Study (Wu and colleagues, New England Journal of Medicine, 2010) tested a random sample of men aged 40 to 79 in eight European centres and set a strict definition of late-onset hypogonadism: at least three sexual symptoms (poor morning erections, low sexual desire and erectile dysfunction) together with low total and free testosterone. About 2 in 100 men met it. The rate climbed with age, from about 1 in 1,000 men in their 40s and 6 in 1,000 in their 50s to about 3 in 100 in their 60s and 5 in 100 in their 70s, and it was higher in men with a larger body-mass index and more health conditions. The NHS likewise describes late-onset hypogonadism as an uncommon, specific condition that is not a normal part of aging, often seen with obesity or type 2 diabetes.

  • About 2 in 100 men

    Aged 40 to 79 met the strict EMAS definition of late-onset hypogonadism.

  • More common with age

    From about 1 in 1,000 men in their 40s to about 5 in 100 in their 70s.

  • Weight and health count

    Rates were higher with a larger body-mass index and more health conditions.

  • Sexual symptoms lead

    The three sexual symptoms were the ones that tracked most closely with low testosterone.

What actually changes in the body

In menopause the change starts in the ovaries. The NIH explains that during perimenopause the ovaries make less estrogen and progesterone, ovulation becomes less frequent and periods eventually stop; the Canadian Menopause Society ties natural menopause to the loss of ovarian follicles. The change is permanent. In men the testes keep making testosterone; the level just sits a little lower each decade. Part of the change is in how testosterone is carried: the Endocrine Society lists aging among the conditions that raise SHBG, the protein that binds testosterone in the blood, so free testosterone (the share not bound to SHBG) can fall more than total testosterone. The Endocrine Society also notes that testosterone is affected by age, obesity, diabetes, sleep apnea, acute illness, nutrition and medications such as opioids and glucocorticoids, which is why two men of the same age can have very different levels.

  • Ovaries wind down

    Follicles run out, estrogen and progesterone fall and ovulation stops.

  • Testes keep working

    Testosterone production carries on through life at a slowly lower level.

  • SHBG rises with age

    More testosterone is bound, so free T can fall more than total T. That is why it gets measured.

  • Health shifts the level

    Obesity, diabetes, sleep apnea, illness and some medications all lower testosterone.

Symptoms: where they overlap and where they differ

The two share more symptoms than people expect. The NHS lists sleep problems, mood changes and low mood, poor memory and concentration, weight gain around the stomach, loss of bone density and reduced sex drive for menopause, alongside the hallmark changes to periods, hot flushes and night sweats, vaginal dryness and more urinary infections. Its list for the “male menopause” covers low sex drive, erectile dysfunction, mood swings and irritability, loss of muscle, a larger belly, low energy, poor sleep and poor concentration. For men, the European Male Ageing Study found the three sexual symptoms (fewer morning erections, low sexual desire and erectile dysfunction) were the ones that tracked with low testosterone. The Endocrine Society also lists hot flushes and sweats among the signs of testosterone deficiency, in a list based on men with severe deficiency, so they are not a typical part of ordinary male aging. The NHS adds that in many midlife men these symptoms have nothing to do with hormones and come from stress, depression, anxiety, poor sleep, diet, inactivity, alcohol or smoking.   • Shared: drive and sleep

    Lower sex drive, poor sleep, low mood and brain fog show up in both.

  • Shared: body changes

    Weight moving to the belly and lower bone density happen in both.

  • Men: sexual signs lead

    Fewer morning erections, low desire and erectile dysfunction track most closely with low T.

  • Often not hormones

    Stress, low mood, sleep, alcohol and inactivity drive many of the same symptoms in men.

How each one is confirmed

Here the two work in opposite directions. For women aged 45 or over with typical symptoms, NICE guidance (NG23) says perimenopause and menopause are identified from symptoms and periods without lab tests, because hormones such as FSH swing too much in the years before menopause to give a useful single reading; an FSH test is kept for women aged 40 to 45 or younger. For men, a blood test is the whole point. The Endocrine Society recommends diagnosing low testosterone only when symptoms line up with unequivocally and consistently low levels, measured fasting in the early morning and repeated to confirm, because testosterone peaks in the morning, food lowers it and about three in ten first results in the low range come back normal on repeat. The American Urological Association uses a total testosterone below 300 ng/dL on two early-morning tests, and the Canadian Urological Association recommends a draw between 7 and 11 a.m. and suggests below 10 nmol/L as a reasonable threshold. Free testosterone, SHBG and LH then show whether the issue is binding, the testes or the brain’s signal. See low testosterone labs to ask for.

  • Menopause: the calendar

    From 45, symptoms and twelve months without a period are usually enough.

  • Low T: early morning

    Fasting, early-morning draws, because testosterone peaks in the morning.

  • Always repeat

    About three in ten first low results are normal on repeat, so one test is not enough.

  • Free T, SHBG and LH

    These explain a borderline result and point to the cause.

What men can do about andropause symptoms

Because male hormonal aging is gradual and strongly tied to health, men have more levers than a fixed timeline suggests. The Canadian Urological Association lists causes to address first: obesity, sleep apnea, acute illness, opioids and glucocorticoids, extreme dieting and extreme exercise. In the European Male Ageing Study’s follow-up, men who lost weight saw testosterone rise in proportion and men who gained weight saw it fall. Short sleep lowers testosterone too, and the American Urological Association recommends lifestyle counselling on weight and activity for every man with low testosterone. The Endocrine Society suggests against routinely prescribing testosterone to all men 65 or older with low levels, which makes the first step a clear picture of your symptoms, labs and habits. If you are weighing testosterone therapy, read TRT or fix the causes first and low T from aging or from weight, sleep and stress.

  • Weight

    Weight loss goes with higher testosterone; weight gain with lower.

  • Sleep and sleep apnea

    Short sleep and untreated sleep apnea both go with lower testosterone.

  • Training, not overtraining

    Regular training supports weight and energy; extreme exercise and extreme dieting are listed causes of low T.

  • Medications and alcohol

    Opioids and glucocorticoids lower testosterone; heavy drinking drives the same symptoms.

Low testosterone and andropause: the overview

Frequently asked questions

Is andropause the same as menopause?

No. Menopause is a defined event: the ovaries run out of egg follicles, estrogen falls and periods stop for good, confirmed once there has been no period for twelve months in a row. The average age is 51 in Canada and the USA. Andropause, or the “male menopause,” is a popular term for something quite different. The NHS describes testosterone falling steadily by about 1 percent a year from around age 30 to 40, with no sudden drop and no end point, and calls the “male menopause” label misleading. The medical term for symptoms plus low testosterone later in life is late-onset hypogonadism.

When does andropause start, and do all men get it?

Testosterone starts drifting down slowly from around age 30 to 40, but that decline on its own is unlikely to cause problems, according to the NHS. Not every man develops symptomatic low testosterone. In the European Male Ageing Study of men aged 40 to 79, about 2 in 100 met a strict definition of late-onset hypogonadism (three sexual symptoms plus low total and free testosterone). That rose from about 1 in 1,000 men in their 40s to about 5 in 100 in their 70s, and it was more common with a higher body-mass index and more health conditions. Menopause, by contrast, happens to every woman.

Can men get hot flashes like women in menopause?

They can, but it is not typical of ordinary aging. The Endocrine Society lists hot flushes and sweats among the signs of testosterone deficiency, in a list it bases on men with severe deficiency. The signs most closely tied to low testosterone in middle-aged and older men are sexual: lower sex drive, fewer morning erections and erectile dysfunction. See low testosterone symptoms in men.

How is andropause diagnosed compared with menopause?

For women aged 45 or over, NICE guidance says menopause is identified from symptoms and periods, usually without blood tests. For men it works the other way round: low testosterone is confirmed only when consistent symptoms line up with low results on two separate fasting, early-morning total testosterone tests. The American Urological Association uses a total testosterone below 300 ng/dL on two early-morning tests, and the Canadian Urological Association suggests below 10 nmol/L as a reasonable threshold. Free testosterone, SHBG and LH help explain borderline results and the cause. See which labs to ask for.

What can men do about andropause symptoms?

Start with the things that move testosterone and symptoms. Guidelines name obesity, sleep apnea, acute illness, opioids, glucocorticoids, extreme dieting and extreme exercise as causes to address first, and in the European Male Ageing Study men who lost weight saw their testosterone rise in proportion. Short sleep, stress, heavy drinking and inactivity drive many of the same symptoms. The Endocrine Society suggests against routinely prescribing testosterone to all men 65 or older with low levels, so the first step is a clear picture of your symptoms, labs and habits.
