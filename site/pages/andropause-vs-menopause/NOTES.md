## Facts and where they come from (all checked Oct 16 2026)
- NHS "The 'male menopause'" (nhs.uk, last reviewed 13 Oct 2022, next review due 13 Oct 2025): "male menopause"/andropause an unhelpful, misleading term because it suggests a sudden midlife drop like the female menopause; "testosterone levels fall as men age, but the decline is steady at about 1% a year from around the age of 30 to 40, and this is unlikely to cause any problems in itself"; late-onset hypogonadism can cause the symptoms but often symptoms are nothing to do with hormones (stress, depression, anxiety, lack of sleep, poor diet, lack of exercise, alcohol, smoking, low self-esteem); LOH "uncommon and specific medical condition that's not a normal part of ageing", particularly in men who are obese or have type 2 diabetes; diagnosed from symptoms + blood tests. Symptom list: depression, low sex drive, ED, mood swings/irritability, loss of muscle, belly fat/gynaecomastia, lack of energy, insomnia/tiredness, poor concentration/memory. (Note: the earlier /low-testosterone-symptoms NOTES said "no figure"; the live page does state "about 1% a year".)
- NHS "Symptoms of menopause and perimenopause" (nhs.uk/conditions/menopause/symptoms/): period changes, hot flushes and night sweats, sleep problems, mood changes/low mood/memory and concentration, weight gain around stomach and upper body, vaginal dryness, more UTIs, palpitations, loss of bone density, reduced libido, joint pain etc.; menopause = no period for 12 months; symptoms usually last 7 to 9 years (not used on page). NHS menopause overview: usually between 45 and 55.
- Canadian Menopause Society, Definition and Stages (canadianmenopausesociety.org/professionals/menopause-hub/definition/): natural menopause = permanent cessation of menstruation due to loss of ovarian follicles, resulting in hypoestrogenism and end of reproductive potential; diagnosed retrospectively after 12 consecutive months of amenorrhea; average age in Canada 51, typically 45-55; surgical/medical menopause often more abrupt and severe symptoms (used as "surgery or some medical treatments can cause a sudden menopause").
- NIH NICHD, About Menopause (nichd.nih.gov/health/topics/menopause/conditioninfo): during perimenopause ovaries produce less estrogen and progesterone, periods less often then stop, ovulation less frequent; menopause = no period in 12 consecutive months; "All women experience menopause, usually between ages 45 and 55"; average age 51; smokers may reach menopause earlier; perimenopause can begin several years earlier.
- NICE NG23 Menopause: identification and management (recommendations 1.3.x): identify perimenopause/menopause without laboratory tests in otherwise healthy people aged 45+ with symptoms; do not use AMH, inhibin, estradiol, antral follicle count, ovarian volume over 45; consider FSH only at 40-45 with symptoms/cycle change or under 40 when menopause suspected. NICE guideline book (NCBI NBK552590): FSH fluctuates considerably in the years leading up to menopause, so blood levels are not helpful in what is a clinical diagnosis.
- EMAS, Wu et al., NEJM 2010;363:123-35 (PubMed abstract via E-utilities + full-text passage from the Imperial College repository copy): 3,369 men aged 40-79, eight European centres, morning T; three sexual symptoms syndromic with low T; LOH definition (>=3 sexual symptoms + total T <11 nmol/L + free T <220 pmol/L); prevalence 2.1% (63/2,966), 0.1% age 40-49, 0.6% 50-59, 3.2% 60-69, 5.1% 70-79; rises with BMI and number of comorbidities. Page wording: "about 2 in 100", "about 1 in 1,000 / 6 in 1,000 / about 3 in 100 / 5 in 100".
- Endocrine Society 2018 (Bhasin et al., JCEM 2018;103:1715, doi 10.1210/jc.2018-00229; abstract via PubMed, full text /tmp/es2018.txt from the hosted JCEM PDF): diagnose only with symptoms/signs + unequivocally and consistently low T; fasting morning total T, repeat to confirm; free T when near lower limit or SHBG altered; rec 1.2 against routine population screening (not used); rec 2.4 "We suggest against routinely prescribing testosterone therapy to all men 65 years or older with low testosterone concentrations" (page: "suggests against routinely prescribing testosterone to all men 65 or older with low levels"); Table 2 lists aging among conditions that increase SHBG; text: T affected by acute illness, nutritional deficiency, opioids, glucocorticoids, age, obesity, diabetes, OSA; morning peak, food lowers T; 30% of initial low results normal on repeat ("about three in ten"); Table 3 lists hot flushes/sweats among signs, based on clinical experience of severely T-deficient men.
- AUA 2018 (Mulhall et al., auanet.org): total T <300 ng/dL on two early-morning tests + symptoms; lifestyle counselling (weight loss/activity) for men with low T (statement wording previously verified from full text for /low-testosterone-age-or-weight).
- CUA 2021 (Grober et al., CUAJ 2021;15(5):E234-43): draw 7-11 a.m.; <10 nmol/L reasonable threshold; correctable causes first: acute illness, obesity, opioids/glucocorticoids, extreme nutritional deficiency, extreme exercise, sleep apnea (+ others); persistent sleep restriction lowers T (source for "short sleep lowers testosterone too").
- EMAS longitudinal, Camacho et al., Eur J Endocrinol 2013;168(3):445-55, doi 10.1530/EJE-12-0890 (PubMed abstract): 2,736 men 40-79, follow-up ~4.4 yr; weight loss associated with a proportional increase and weight gain a proportional decrease in testosterone and SHBG; annualised SHBG +0.56 nmol/L, total T -0.1 nmol/L. Changes in physical activity were NOT significantly associated with HPT changes, so the page credits activity only via AUA counselling and "supports weight and energy".
