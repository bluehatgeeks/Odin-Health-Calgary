What are the side effects of TRT?

Short answer

The most common side effect of testosterone replacement therapy is a rise in red blood cells (hematocrit). Acne, oily skin, a small PSA rise and lower sperm production also have a clear link, while breast swelling, balding and worse sleep apnea are uncommon, according to the Endocrine Society. In the large TRAVERSE trial, testosterone gel did not raise the rate of heart attack or stroke. Most side effects show up on labs early. Which TRT side effects are real, and how common are they?

The Endocrine Society’s 2018 guideline sorts the risks of TRT into three groups. A clear link: erythrocytosis (too many red blood cells), acne and oily skin, finding a hidden prostate cancer, growth of an existing metastatic prostate cancer, and lower sperm production. Uncommon, with weak evidence: breast enlargement (gynecomastia), male pattern balding in men with a family tendency, and new or worse obstructive sleep apnea. Tied to the form: injections can bring swings in mood or libido and injection-site pain, gels can pass to a partner or child through skin contact, and patches often irritate the skin. How often? In a 2005 meta-analysis of 19 randomized trials in men 45 and older (Calof and colleagues), men on testosterone were nearly four times as likely as men on placebo to reach a hematocrit above 50 percent (odds ratio 3.69), and prostate events of any kind were found more often (odds ratio 1.78). Rates of heart events, sleep apnea and death did not differ. If you are still sorting out whether your symptoms point to low T, start with low testosterone symptoms.

TRT and hematocrit: the red blood cell effect

Testosterone raises hemoglobin and hematocrit, and the Endocrine Society notes the rise grows with the dose and blood level and is larger in older men. In a 2010 meta-analysis of 51 studies (Fernández-Balsells and colleagues), hematocrit rose by about 3 points on average. Erythrocytosis (hematocrit above 54 percent) is the most frequent adverse event in testosterone trials, and in the guideline’s own meta-analysis it was about eight times more likely than on placebo (relative risk 8.14). The form matters: in a 2023 randomized trial (Rivero and colleagues), hematocrit climbed from 42.7 to 46.6 percent after four months of cypionate injections but did not change significantly on nasal gel. Why it matters: in a large health-records study (Ory and colleagues, 2022), men on testosterone whose hematocrit reached 52 percent or more had more heart attacks, strokes and blood clots in the first year than men whose hematocrit stayed normal (5.15 versus 3.87 percent). The guideline’s rule: check hematocrit before starting, at three to six months, then yearly; take extra care if it starts above 48 percent (50 percent at high altitude); if it passes 54 percent, pause until it is back in range, check for low oxygen and sleep apnea, and restart at a lower dose. Blood donation (phlebotomy) also works, and the Canadian Urological Association lists a lower dose, a break, a different form or blood donation when hematocrit stays at 55 percent or higher. Hematocrit belongs on the labs to ask for list before you start.

Does TRT cause acne, hair loss or breast changes?

Acne and oily skin have a clear link with testosterone, and together with breast tenderness they are among the most common drug-related complaints, says the Endocrine Society. Hair loss is less certain: the guideline lists male pattern balding as uncommon, with weak evidence, mostly in men with a family tendency. Breast enlargement is also uncommon. Breast symptoms tie back to estradiol, because part of your testosterone is converted to estradiol (more so in body fat, the Canadian Urological Association notes), and the CUA advises measuring estradiol before treatment in men with breast symptoms or gynecomastia. The form makes a difference here too: in the 2023 Rivero trial, estradiol rose with injections but not with nasal gel. If a breakout, thinning or breast tenderness shows up, your estradiol, the dose, its timing and the form are the first things to review.

TRT, fertility and testicle size

Testosterone from outside the body tells the pituitary to cut LH and FSH, the two signals that drive the testes. In a 2005 study in healthy men (Coviello and colleagues), weekly injections pushed LH and FSH down to 5 and 3 percent of baseline and cut testosterone inside the testes by 94 percent within three weeks. With less of their own testosterone, the testes make fewer sperm and get smaller: in a 1981 study (Palacios and colleagues), testicular volume fell by about 16 to 19 percent after four months of injections and returned to normal size after they stopped. In a World Health Organization contraceptive trial (Lancet, 1990), 65 percent of 271 healthy men had no sperm in their semen by six months of weekly injections; after stopping, sperm counts took a median of just under four months to recover to 20 million per mL. That is why the Endocrine Society advises against TRT for men who want a child in the next six to twelve months, and the Canadian Urological Association points men who want to keep their fertility toward options that raise their own testosterone, such as hCG or clomiphene. For recovery and options in depth, read TRT and fertility.

TRT and heart risk: what the TRAVERSE trial found

TRAVERSE (Lincoff and colleagues, New England Journal of Medicine, 2023) was built to settle the heart question. It enrolled 5,246 men aged 45 to 80 with low-T symptoms, two fasting testosterone levels under 300 ng/dL, and existing heart disease or high heart risk, and gave them testosterone gel or a placebo gel. Over close to three years of follow-up on average, a heart attack, stroke or cardiovascular death occurred in 7.0 percent of the testosterone group and 7.3 percent of the placebo group (hazard ratio 0.96), so testosterone was noninferior. The trial did find more atrial fibrillation, acute kidney injury and pulmonary embolism with testosterone, and a TRAVERSE fracture study (Snyder and colleagues, 2024) found more fractures, not fewer (3.50 versus 2.46 percent). In February 2025 the US FDA removed the boxed-warning language about heart outcomes and added the TRAVERSE results to every testosterone label, and it added a blood pressure warning because ambulatory monitoring studies showed blood pressure rising with all testosterone products. In June 2026 it asked for further label updates, including revised prostate safety information. On cholesterol, the 2010 meta-analysis found a small drop in HDL. How this fits the bigger choice is covered in TRT or fix the causes first.

TRT and the prostate: PSA and cancer risk

Testosterone raises PSA a little. In a systematic review cited by the Endocrine Society, PSA rose by about 0.3 ng/mL in young men with low T and 0.44 ng/mL in older men; in the Testosterone Trials, a rise of more than 1.4 ng/mL happened in 2.4 percent of treated men at three months and 4.7 percent at one year, against 1.6 and 0.6 percent on placebo. Does TRT cause prostate cancer? In the TRAVERSE prostate analysis (Bhasin and colleagues, JAMA Network Open, 2023) of 5,204 men screened to exclude high prostate risk, high-grade prostate cancer occurred in 0.19 percent on testosterone and 0.12 percent on placebo, a difference that was not statistically significant, and rates of any prostate cancer, urinary retention, biopsies and procedures did not differ. The Canadian Urological Association cites a meta-analysis of 22 randomized trials with 2,351 men that found no higher prostate cancer risk. The caution is for cancer that is already there: the Endocrine Society says testosterone can bring a hidden cancer to light and helps metastatic prostate cancer grow, so it advises against starting with a PSA above 4 ng/mL (above 3 for higher-risk men, such as African American men or men with a first-degree relative with prostate cancer) until it has been checked, and calls for a closer prostate work-up if PSA rises by more than 1.4 ng/mL in the first year, passes 4 ng/mL, or the prostate exam changes.

TRT, sleep apnea and blood pressure

The Endocrine Society lists new or worse obstructive sleep apnea as uncommon, with weak evidence, and the 2005 Calof meta-analysis found no significant difference in sleep apnea between testosterone and placebo. Even so, the guideline advises against starting testosterone while severe sleep apnea is untreated, and it pairs a high hematocrit with a check for sleep apnea and low oxygen. Sleep apnea can also lower testosterone on its own (the Canadian Urological Association lists it among causes that can be corrected), so it is one of the first things we look at. Blood pressure is the newer item: the FDA now says testosterone raises blood pressure across all products, so a home or pharmacy reading belongs in every check-in. What gets monitored on TRT, and when

The Endocrine Society sets a standard plan: review symptoms and side effects, and check testosterone and hematocrit at three to six months, at one year and then yearly. Testosterone should sit in the mid-normal range; with cypionate or enanthate injections it is measured midway between shots, and a result above 600 or below 350 ng/dL means the dose or interval needs adjusting. Men 55 to 69, and men 40 to 69 at higher risk, who choose prostate monitoring have a PSA and prostate exam before starting and again at three to twelve months, then follow normal screening. Men with osteoporosis get a bone density scan after one to two years. The Canadian Urological Association’s schedule is close: baseline, three months, six months, then yearly. Most TRT side effects are manageable

Most of these effects depend on dose and blood level, and they show up on labs before they cause trouble. The guideline tools are simple: lower the dose or change the interval, switch the form (the CUA notes nasal gel may suppress sperm production less, and in the Rivero trial it left hematocrit and estradiol unchanged), take a break until hematocrit settles, or donate blood. A man who wants children plans differently from the start. And the American Urological Association (2018, reviewed 2024) says a man is a candidate for testosterone therapy only when low levels come with symptoms, so for many men the best way to avoid side effects is to fix what is lowering testosterone first: weight, sleep, alcohol, medications and stress.
