What are the symptoms of low testosterone in men?

Short answer

The signs most closely tied to low testosterone are sexual: lower sex drive, fewer morning erections and erectile dysfunction. Many men also notice fatigue, less muscle and strength, more belly fat, low mood, irritability or poor focus. Those signs overlap with poor sleep, sleep apnea, thyroid problems, depression, weight gain and some medications, so low T is confirmed when symptoms line up with two early-morning total testosterone tests. Sexual symptoms: the clearest signal

The best evidence on which symptoms really track with testosterone comes from the European Male Ageing Study (Wu and colleagues, New England Journal of Medicine, 2010). Researchers surveyed a random sample of 3,369 men aged 40 to 79 across eight European centres and measured testosterone in morning blood samples. Poor morning erections, low sexual desire and erectile dysfunction were the three symptoms with a syndromic link to lower testosterone, and the more of those three a man reported, the lower his testosterone tended to be. Fatigue, low mood and trouble with vigorous activity were also related to testosterone, but less specifically. The Endocrine Society’s 2018 guideline likewise lists reduced sexual desire and activity and decreased spontaneous erections among the symptoms most suggestive of low testosterone. Erectile dysfunction on its own has many other causes, including stress, anxiety, smoking and heart and blood vessel health, so it is one clue rather than the whole picture.

Physical symptoms: energy, muscle, belly fat and bone

The Endocrine Society and the Canadian Urological Association both list physical changes that go with low testosterone: decreased energy and motivation, reduced muscle bulk and strength, increased body fat (the Canadian guideline names visceral fat, the deep fat around the belly), and lower bone mineral density. The Endocrine Society also lists height loss and low-trauma fractures, breast discomfort or enlargement, hot flushes and sweats, and mild unexplained anemia. These are real signals, but most of them are less specific than the sexual symptoms: fatigue and a growing waistline have many everyday drivers, and extra belly fat itself lowers testosterone. Low bone density usually causes no symptoms at all until a scan or a fracture finds it.

Mood, focus and sleep

Low testosterone can show up in how you feel and think. The Endocrine Society lists decreased self-confidence, feeling sad or blue or a persistently low mood, poor concentration and memory, and sleep disturbance or increased sleepiness. In community surveys it cites, irritability and depressed mood were associated with low testosterone, and the Canadian Urological Association adds changes in mood, insomnia and irritability to its list. The catch is that every one of these is also a classic sign of depression, chronic stress, anxiety or poor sleep. The NHS notes that in many midlife men these symptoms have nothing to do with hormones, which is why a mood or focus change is a reason to look closer, not proof of low T.

Why these symptoms overlap with other causes

The American Urological Association’s testosterone guideline makes the core point plainly: the symptoms linked to low testosterone are very non-specific and can come from other conditions, such as chronic fatigue, chronic stress or a depressed state. The Canadian Urological Association recommends ruling out conditions with an overlapping symptom picture, naming depression, an underactive thyroid and sleep disorders, and lists causes to address first, including obesity, sleep apnea, acute illness, opioids and glucocorticoids such as prednisone, extreme dieting and extreme exercise. The Endocrine Society adds that testosterone levels themselves are affected by acute illness, nutrition, age, obesity, diabetes and sleep apnea. Some of these mimic low T; others actually lower it. Either way, sorting them out is the first job. See low T from aging or from weight, sleep and stress.

Symptoms plus two early-morning tests

Because the symptoms overlap so much, guidelines in both countries agree that low testosterone is a combination: consistent symptoms plus low blood levels, confirmed on more than one test. The Endocrine Society recommends a fasting, early-morning total testosterone test, repeated to confirm, because testosterone peaks in the morning and food lowers it; in that guideline, about three in ten men whose first result looked low had a normal result on repeat. The American Urological Association uses a total testosterone below 300 ng/dL on two separate early-morning tests, alongside symptoms. The Canadian Urological Association recommends a morning draw between 7 and 11 a.m., suggests below 10 nmol/L as a reasonable threshold, and notes that some men have symptoms above it. When total testosterone is borderline, or SHBG may be high or low, free testosterone gives a clearer read; LH helps show whether the testes or the brain’s signal is the issue, and prolactin is added when LH is low.
