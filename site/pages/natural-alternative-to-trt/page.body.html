Confirm it’s really low T first

Before you change anything, make sure the number is real. Testosterone is highest in the morning and varies from day to day, so one afternoon result can mislead. The Endocrine Society guideline (2018) recommends calling it low only when symptoms come with consistently low results: a fasting, early-morning total testosterone, repeated on a second morning.

  • {{CI:sun:0}}

    Test in the morning, after a normal night

    Book an early-morning, fasting blood draw after a typical night’s sleep, not after a night shift or a run of short nights. In a study of 10 young men, one week of five-hour nights cut daytime testosterone by 10 to 15 percent (Leproult and Van Cauter, 2011).

  • {{CI:calendar:1}}

    Repeat it if it’s low

    One low result is a starting point, not a diagnosis. A second morning test shows whether your level is consistently low.

  • {{CI:tube:2}}

    Ask for the right labs

    Start with total testosterone, then free testosterone, SHBG, LH and FSH, and often prolactin, estradiol and hematocrit. Our guide to low testosterone labs to ask for explains what each one is for.

  • {{CI:gauge:3}}

    Work out your free testosterone

    When total testosterone is borderline or SHBG may be unusual, the guideline advises checking free testosterone. Our free testosterone calculator estimates it from total T, SHBG and albumin.

If it is confirmed, guidelines recommend looking for the cause next, and that is where a natural plan starts. We help you decide which tests to ask your doctor for and go through the results with you in plain language.

Supplements and herbs: what the research shows

Supplements are one lever in a natural plan, not the plan itself. Here is what human studies show for four names men ask about most.

  • {{CI:sun:0}}

    Vitamin D

    In an Austrian trial of 54 overweight men on a weight-loss program whose vitamin D started in the deficient range, a year of vitamin D raised total testosterone from 10.7 to 13.4 nmol/L (Pilz, 2011). Two later 12-week trials, in men with normal and with low testosterone, found no effect (Lerchbaum, 2017 and 2019), and a 2026 meta-analysis found no clear effect (Paez-Allendes, 2026).

  • {{CI:drop:1}}

    Zinc

    Severe and moderate zinc deficiency goes with low testosterone. In nine older men who were marginally zinc-deficient, six months of zinc raised testosterone from 8.3 to 16.0 nmol/L (Prasad, 1996). The benefit shows up mainly when you are low.

  • {{CI:leaf:2}}

    Ashwagandha

    A 2026 meta-analysis of 23 randomized trials with 1,706 people found that ashwagandha lowered cortisol, a stress hormone, and raised testosterone in men by about 57 ng/dL on average (about 2 nmol/L, our conversion), but not in women. The authors called for more standardized trials (Fornalik, 2026).

  • {{CI:flame:3}}

    Fenugreek

    A 2026 meta-analysis of randomized, placebo-controlled trials in men found a small positive effect on total testosterone across six trials, no steady effect on free testosterone across five, and rated the certainty of the evidence as very low (Yang, 2026).

Who a natural approach suits, and who chooses TRT

This is your decision, and it often isn’t either/or. What matters is matching the approach to your cause, your goals and your timeline.

{{CI:leaf:0}}

A natural approach often suits men who

  • Were told their labs look “normal” or borderline but still don’t feel right
  • Can see sleep, stress, weight, alcohol or training playing a part
  • Want children in the near term: the Endocrine Society guideline advises against starting testosterone therapy in men planning fertility soon
  • Would rather not commit to ongoing therapy and monitoring
{{CI:pills:1}}

Some men choose TRT when

  • Low testosterone is confirmed on repeat morning tests and symptoms persist
  • The cause is in the testes or pituitary rather than daily life
  • They have already worked on the causes and want to go further
