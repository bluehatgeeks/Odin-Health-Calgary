Can low testosterone cause insomnia, and how does sleep affect T?

Short answer

Testosterone rises while you sleep, so short, broken or badly timed sleep can pull it down — and low T and poor sleep share many of the same symptoms. Low T is linked with lighter, more broken sleep, but studies have not shown it causes insomnia on its own. In a JAMA study, a week of five-hour nights lowered young men’s daytime testosterone by 10 to 15 percent. Sleep apnea travels with extra weight and lower T, and night shifts move the rise to whenever you sleep. Can low testosterone cause insomnia?

Low testosterone and insomnia often show up together, but the research has not shown that low T on its own causes insomnia. The stronger evidence runs the other way: short, broken sleep lowers testosterone, and extra weight and sleep apnea drive both. Testosterone therapy is not a proven sleep fix, and in men with untreated sleep apnea it can make breathing at night worse.

In the largest sleep-lab study, 1,312 men aged 65 and older in the Osteoporotic Fractures in Men study (Barrett-Connor and colleagues, Journal of Clinical Endocrinology & Metabolism, 2008), men with lower testosterone had lower sleep efficiency, more awakenings in the night, less slow-wave (deep) sleep and more sleep apnea, yet most of those links faded once body fat was accounted for. In 1,274 men over 65 in Hong Kong (Auyeung and colleagues, 2015), insomnia complaints were not linked with testosterone, while testosterone rose with sleep length up to about 10 hours a night. A 2024 study of US health records (Agrawal and colleagues, International Journal of Impotence Research) found testosterone deficiency more often in men aged 40 to 70 with insomnia (odds ratio 1.74), sleep apnea (1.66) and circadian rhythm sleep disorder (2.63) than in matched men, an association that cannot show which comes first. Trials of testosterone and sleep are small and point in different directions: one Japanese trial in men without apnea found better sleep scores, while two Australian trials found that testosterone shortened sleep or worsened night-time breathing, at least early on. The Endocrine Society (2018) advises against starting testosterone therapy while severe sleep apnea is untreated.

Why testosterone depends on sleep

Testosterone follows a daily rhythm: it climbs and peaks during sleep, then drifts to its low point in the late afternoon, with smaller pulses about every 90 minutes driven by LH, the brain’s signal to the testes. A 2014 review by Wittert in the Asian Journal of Andrology concludes that the rise depends on sleep itself rather than the clock, and needs at least three hours of sleep with a normal structure. Sleep-lab work by Luboshitzky and colleagues (Journal of Clinical Endocrinology & Metabolism, 2001) tied the overnight rise to the first REM period: when sleep was broken into short naps, the rise came hours later, and in men who reached no REM sleep it was much weaker. In a 2005 study by Axelsson and colleagues, testosterone in seven young men rose the longer they slept and fell the longer they stayed awake. The amount, depth and timing of your sleep all feed into the number on your lab report.

  • It rises in sleep

    Testosterone climbs while you sleep and falls while you are awake, whatever the clock says.

  • It needs a solid block

    The rise needs at least three hours of sleep with a normal structure, so broken nights blunt it.

  • REM sleep matters

    The overnight rise lines up with the first REM period. Men who missed REM sleep had a much weaker rise.

  • Lowest in late afternoon

    Levels are highest after sleep and lowest late in the day, which is why labs are drawn in the morning.

What short sleep does to testosterone

In the best-known test, Leproult and Van Cauter at the University of Chicago (JAMA, 2011) had 10 healthy men with an average age of 24 spend three nights with 10 hours in bed, then eight nights with five hours in bed. Their sleep fell from just under nine hours to under five. After the seventh short night, testosterone across the waking day (8 a.m. to 10 p.m.) averaged 16.5 nmol/L, compared with 18.4 nmol/L when rested, and the gap was widest between 2 and 10 p.m. The authors put the drop at 10 to 15 percent, and the men’s vigour scores fell from 28 to 19 over the week. Older men show the same pattern in everyday life: in Penev’s study of 12 healthy men aged 64 to 74 (Sleep, 2007), the amount of nighttime sleep predicted morning total and free testosterone. The Canadian Urological Association (2021) sums it up: persistent sleep restriction lowers testosterone by about 10 to 15 percent, and a structured sleep program may offset it. Sleep apnea and low testosterone

In obstructive sleep apnea, the airway keeps closing during sleep. A 2019 meta-analysis by Cignarelli and colleagues describes it as a frequent complication of obesity that has been linked with low testosterone, and the two share symptoms: the US National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute lists loud snoring, breathing that stops and starts, gasping, daytime sleepiness, and sexual dysfunction or lower libido among its signs. The Endocrine Society (2018) lists sleep apnea among the conditions that affect testosterone, and the Canadian Urological Association (2021) puts it on its list of potentially correctable causes. The research points to weight as the shared driver. Wittert’s 2014 review found that apnea had no direct effect on testosterone after adjusting for age and obesity, while weight loss raised testosterone in proportion to the weight lost. The Cignarelli meta-analysis (12 studies, 388 men) found that CPAP on its own did not significantly change total testosterone. The Endocrine Society also advises against starting testosterone therapy while severe sleep apnea is untreated, so checking for it belongs in every low-T workup. Weighing your options? Shift work, night sleep and testosterone

Night and rotating shifts push your main sleep into the day, when it is easier to cut short. Testosterone seems to follow sleep rather than the clock: in Axelsson’s study, testosterone rose during an eight-hour daytime sleep (7 a.m. to 3 p.m.) much as it did during night sleep, from about 17 to 26 nmol/L by day and about 15 to 25 nmol/L by night. The problem is getting enough of that sleep. In a 2020 study of 2,571 men seen at a US andrology clinic (Balasubramanian and colleagues, Urology), about one in four worked nonstandard shifts, and nearly a third of those were at high risk of shift work sleep disorder. That group had more low-T symptoms and testosterone about 100 ng/dL lower than shift workers without it, after adjusting for age and other conditions. The NHLBI’s advice for shift workers: make more time for sleep, keep lights bright at work, limit shift changes, keep caffeine to the first part of the shift, and block light and noise for daytime sleep. Testing: morning labs after a normal night

Because testosterone peaks in the morning and food lowers it, the Endocrine Society (2018) recommends a fasting, early-morning total testosterone test, repeated to confirm. The Canadian Urological Association recommends drawing it between 7 and 11 a.m., and the American Urological Association defines low testosterone as symptoms plus a total testosterone below 300 ng/dL on two early-morning tests. Sleep matters for reading the result: a draw after a run of short nights, straight off a night shift or with untreated sleep apnea may sit below your usual baseline, so note your sleep alongside each result. When results are borderline, free testosterone, SHBG and LH help explain them. You can estimate free testosterone from your total testosterone, SHBG and albumin with our free testosterone calculator. Sleep habits that support healthy testosterone

The NHLBI recommends 7 to 9 hours of sleep a night for adults and lists habits that make it easier: go to bed and wake up at the same time every day, keeping weekends within about an hour; use the hour before bed for quiet time away from bright screens and hard exercise; avoid heavy meals and alcohol close to bedtime; keep caffeine and nicotine out of the evening, since caffeine can last up to eight hours; get outside and be active during the day; and keep your bedroom quiet, cool and dark. The Canadian Urological Association notes that a structured sleep program may offset the effect of limited sleep on testosterone. Losing extra weight raises testosterone in proportion to the weight lost, and the CUA notes that exercise, including resistance training, brings modest rises. Frequently asked questions

Does low testosterone cause insomnia?

Not on its own, as far as the research shows. In a 2008 study of 1,312 men aged 65 and older, men with lower testosterone slept less efficiently, woke more often in the night and had less deep sleep, but most of that link was explained by body fat. In a 2015 study of 1,274 men over 65, insomnia complaints and taking a long time to fall asleep were not linked with testosterone at all. A 2024 US health-records study found testosterone deficiency was more common in men with insomnia, which shows the two travel together, not which causes which. The better-proven direction runs the other way: short, broken sleep lowers testosterone. So we start with your sleep, your weight and the signs of sleep apnea, alongside two morning labs.

Will testosterone therapy help me sleep?

The evidence is mixed, and it is not a proven fix for insomnia. In a Japanese trial of 48 men with low testosterone and sleep problems but no sleep apnea (the EARTH study, 2018), a year of testosterone therapy improved sleep-disturbance scores compared with no treatment. But in 17 healthy men over 60, short-term high-dose testosterone cut sleep by about an hour and made breathing during sleep worse (2003), and in 67 men with obesity and severe sleep apnea, testosterone worsened night-time oxygen drops early in treatment, an effect that faded by the end of the trial (2012). The Endocrine Society advises against starting testosterone therapy while severe sleep apnea is untreated. That is why checking for apnea and fixing the sleep itself come first; we build that plan with you and track it with repeat labs.

Does poor sleep lower testosterone?

It can. In a University of Chicago study published in JAMA in 2011, 10 healthy young men spent about a week in bed only five hours a night. Their daytime testosterone fell from an average of 18.4 to 16.5 nmol/L, a drop of 10 to 15 percent, and their sense of vigour fell night by night. In older men, a 2007 study in the journal Sleep found that men who slept less at night had lower morning testosterone. The Canadian Urological Association (2021) notes that a structured sleep program may offset the effect of limited sleep. We build that sleep plan with you and re-check your labs to see the change.

Can sleep apnea cause low testosterone?

Sleep apnea and low testosterone often show up together, and extra body weight is the main link. A 2014 review in the Asian Journal of Andrology found that obstructive sleep apnea had no direct effect on testosterone once age and weight were accounted for, while weight loss raised testosterone in proportion to the weight lost. A 2019 meta-analysis of 12 studies with 388 men found CPAP on its own did not significantly change testosterone. Loud snoring, gasping at night and daytime sleepiness are worth a sleep study, and the Endocrine Society advises against starting testosterone therapy while severe sleep apnea is untreated. We help you map those signs and build the weight, training and sleep plan.

Can low testosterone cause poor sleep?

The link can run both ways. The same 2014 review notes that low testosterone may affect overall sleep quality. And many symptoms blamed on low T, such as low energy, lower sex drive, poor concentration and daytime sleepiness, can also come from sleep loss alone, as the JAMA study authors point out. That overlap is why we look at your sleep and your labs together before deciding what is driving your symptoms.

I work nights. When should I get my testosterone tested?

Guidelines ask for an early-morning, fasting draw because testosterone peaks in the morning. In a 2005 study of seven young men, testosterone rose during sleep and fell while awake, and it rose during daytime sleep much as it did at night. So for shift workers, the timing of your main sleep matters for reading the result: tell whoever orders your labs about your rotation and test on a stable stretch. In a 2020 study of 2,571 men at an andrology clinic, shift workers with shift work sleep disorder had lower testosterone and more low-T symptoms. See our shift work page.

How much sleep should men aim for?

The US National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute says adults need 7 to 9 hours a night. Its tips: keep the same bed and wake times every day, use the hour before bed for quiet time away from bright screens, avoid heavy meals and alcohol close to bedtime, keep caffeine and nicotine away from the evening, get outside and be active during the day, and keep your bedroom quiet, cool and dark.
